Everyone who reaches 100 has crossed an entire century in their own way. When they are gone, their stories go with them.

This book began on an ordinary Sunday, when Mr. Onofre remembered the name of the medicine his family took during the Spanish Flu. Two months later, his grandson asked again. He gave the exact same name.

The roads of Mr. Onofre

A repair shop, a lumber truck, a taxi, a city bus. More than 70 years behind the wheel, marked here by his age, like mile markers along a highway.

  1. 16years
    1937

    Runs away from the barbershop to chase the truck

    His father wanted him to be a barber and set him to shining shoes in a shop in Raul Soares. Whenever the barber took his after-lunch nap, he slipped out and climbed into the cab of the lumber truck.

    “Mine was never the scissors. It was the steering wheel.”
  2. 24years
    1945

    Hauls lumber and soldiers during the war

    With no gasoline left in Brazil, he drives on wood gas and fuel alcohol between Nanuque and the port of Caravelas, in Bahia. One night a lantern gets too close to the fuel: his back is burned and he spends 90 days in bed. Then he goes back to the road.

    “Life was hard, but I was harder than it.”
  3. 27years
    1948

    Gets his driver’s card at the police station

    Driver’s licenses did not exist yet. The test was one drive around the block in a 1929 Chevrolet, watched by a soldier.

    “This young man drives just fine. Give him his card.”
  4. 28years
    1949

    Moves to Belo Horizonte and starts driving buses

    He joins the Viação Vitória bus company, where he also trains new drivers. Barroca, Gameleira, Anchieta, Coração de Jesus: every neighborhood a story.

    “You want to learn to be a driver? Then learn respect first.”
  5. 31years
    July 19, 1952

    Marries Nilza

    A courtship of long talks on the sidewalk in Vila Santa Lúcia. Five children followed, all of them put through college by his double shifts at the wheel.

    “She never left my side. She was a true companion.”
  6. 75years
    1996

    Named Model Driver of Minas Gerais, first place out of 480

    Never got a ticket. Never ran a red light. He waited when it turned red, and was the first to go when it turned green.

    “I never crashed, never hit anyone… I only got hit.”
  7. 80years
    2000

    Steps off the bus because his children asked

    “Dad, that’s enough. Time to rest!” And I listened. I stopped.
  8. 105years
    2026

    Tells it all, down to street names and house numbers

    In weekly conversations with his grandson, the stories became 15 chapters. Some moved him deeply. After one of them he cried and then fell asleep, as if he had gone back to that place.

    “That’s it. But only for today.”

What he learned in 105 years

People ask him how you get to 100. His answer has no secret in it: it is about how you conduct yourself.

It’s not a miracle

“Yes, it is possible to live past one hundred. But for that, you have to take care of your health in a first-class way.”

Discipline, exercise and simple food, every day.

Soda instead of liquor

“The others ordered a shot of cachaça, a beer. Me, I ordered my Guaraná soda.”

He watched his after-work companions fall ill, one by one.

Don’t run the red light

“No drinking, no running red lights, no disrespecting others.”

On the road and in life, the same rule.

Keep what has a story

“Throw it away? No way. This shoe has a story.”

The white shoes he bought decades ago got new soles and still come out for special occasions.

Knowing when to stop is courage too

“I just saw something that scared me. Really scared me.”

After witnessing a robbery, he quit his job carrying payroll cash from the bank and went back to the peace of driving a bus.

Two medals

“One from the road, as Model Driver. And another, even more important, as head of the family.”

Five children with college degrees. To him, the greatest victory.

These are life lessons in his own words, not medical advice.

Who is the centenarian in your family?

This book started with one question on a Sunday. It can start that way in your home too: a grandfather, a great-grandmother, a 98-year-old neighbor who remembers everything.

Record the conversation on your phone. Write down names, streets, prices, nicknames. Ask again two months later. What they repeat is memory, and memory is the first thing we lose when nobody writes it down.

Questions to start with next Sunday

  • What was your first job, and how much did you earn?
  • How did you meet the person you married?
  • What was the day you thought you would not make it?
  • What object have you kept all these years, and why?
  • What did you do all your life to get here in good shape?
